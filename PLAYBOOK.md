# Community-Led Growth Playbook for B2B SaaS
**Topic:** Community-Led Growth for B2B SaaS  
**Based on:** Research from 10 practitioners  
**Date:** 2025-04-28

---

## What Is This Playbook? 

This is a practical SOP (Standard Operating Procedure) for B2B SaaS companies
that want to grow through community rather than cold outreach or paid ads.
Every recommendation is sourced from a practitioner who has actually done it.

---

## Core Argument

Community-led growth works because trust compounds. In the age when everyone can build
the same product with AI, the real differentiator is relationships.
(Source: Brendan Beh, LinkedIn, 2025 — "When everyone can ship, the real
constraints become trust and distribution. And the only place trust compounds
at scale is inside communities.")

---

## The Playbook

### Phase 1 — Don't Call It Community

When pitching community-led growth internally to executives who are skeptical, we need 
to avoid using the word "community" entirely. Instead, frame it around outcomes
they already understand, such as: product feedback loops, user-generated content, and
ambassador-style brand amplification.
(Source: Patrick Woods, LinkedIn, 2025 — "We're increasingly not saying the
C Word at all, but rather focusing on the powerful outcomes that emerge from
communities.")

**Why this works:** Executives approve budgets for things they understand.
Once you get the budget and show results, the community builds itself.

---

### Phase 2 — Build Around a Real Problem, Not Your Brand

The most common mistake is building a brand community instead of a problem
community. People don't gather around your logo — they gather around shared
difficulties. 

Do not acquire existing communities or rebrand them. Support them instead.
The individuality of each community is what makes the network valuable.
(Source: Brendan Beh, LinkedIn, 2025 — "We don't acquire communities, nor
do we rebrand them. The individuality of each community is exactly what makes
the network valuable.")

**Practical step:** Before launching, identify the specific problem your
ideal customer has that has nothing to do with your product. Build the
community around that problem. Your product becomes the natural solution.

---

### Phase 3 — Warm Intros Over Cold Outreach

Data shows that meetings sourced from referrals and social represent only
12% of total meetings for most B2B sales teams, yet they drive 33% of
closed-won revenue.
(Source: Mac Reddin, LinkedIn, 2025 — survey of 1,000+ sales leaders)

The reason companies don't systematize warm intros:
1. 55% of sales leaders offer no system or training for network-sourced leads
2. 24% of reps are afraid of crossing a professional boundary
3. 48.5% of leaders think their team is already relationship-led — but the
   data says otherwise

**SOP for warm intros:**
1. After every closed deal, ask: "Who else do you know with this problem?"
2. Give the referrer a simple script so they don't have to think
3. Track referral pipeline separately from cold pipeline in your CRM
4. Report on referral close rate vs cold outreach close rate monthly

---

### Phase 4 — Build in Public with Real Numbers

The most effective community content shares specific metrics, failures, and
lessons. Lloyed Lobo's book sold 7,000 copies in its first few days partly
because he had spent years sharing real numbers publicly, building an
audience that trusted him before the book launched.
(Source: Lloyed Lobo, LinkedIn, 2025)

Jono Bacon's People Powered sold 16,500+ copies and reached the top 5-7%
of all traditionally published business books — after 60+ rejections from
agents.
(Source: Jono Bacon, LinkedIn, EOY 2025)

**What this means for your SaaS:**
Share your community metrics publicly — member count, engagement rate,
revenue attributed to community. Companies that share numbers build
faster-growing communities than those that don't.

---

### Phase 5 — Structure Your Community with the Orbit Model

Use the Orbit Model (developed by Patrick Woods at Orbit) to segment your
community members by engagement level rather than treating everyone the same.

The four levels:
- **Observers** — aware of you, however is not yet engaged
- **Participants** — occasionally engages
- **Contributors** — regularly adds value
- **Ambassadors** — evangelizes on your behalf

**SOP:**
1. Map your existing users to these four levels
2. Create specific programs for each level (not one-size-fits-all)
3. Focus energy on moving Participants to Contributors — that's the highest
   leverage transition
4. Never try to turn Observers into Ambassadors directly — it doesn't work

---

### Phase 6 — Use Developer Relations as a Community Engine

For technical B2B SaaS products, Developer Relations is one of the most
underutilized community-led growth levers. DevRel creates content, runs
office hours, hosts webinars, and builds the documentation that turns
users into advocates.
(Source: Mary Thengvall, Camunda Migration Taskforce, LinkedIn 2025 —
published blog posts, hosted webinars, launched office hours series,
built migration landing page — all within 3 weeks)

**SOP for DevRel-driven community:**
1. Identify the top 3 technical problems your users have to deal with 
2. Create one piece of content per problem (blog post, video, guide)
3. Host a live office hours session monthly — record it
4. Build a resource landing page that aggregates everything
5. Ask the community what to create next

---

### Phase 7 — Measure What Actually Matters

Use the SPACES model (developed by CMX) to decide what to measure based
on what type of community you're building:
- **S**upport
- **P**roduct
- **A**cquisition
- **C**ontribution
- **E**ngagement
- **S**uccess of customers

Most SaaS companies only measure Engagement (likes, comments, posts) when
they should be measuring Acquisition (pipeline from community) and
Success (retention of community members vs non-members).

**Minimum viable community metrics dashboard:**
- % of new pipeline sourced from community
- Retention rate: community members vs non-members
- Number of active contributors per month
- NPS of community members vs non-members

## Where Experts Disagree

### 1. Brand-first vs Problem-first community building

**Brendan Beh** argues strongly against brand-first communities. He says
companies that build a brand on top of grassroots communities extract value
upward, give nothing back, and eventually kill the culture. He built AI.SEA
specifically to avoid this — supporting existing communities rather than
absorbing them.

**Lloyed Lobo** argues that building a strong brand IS the community strategy.
His 13 rules include making your brand unforgettable and turning community into
a cult-like following — language that implies the brand is central, not
secondary.

**My take:** Beh is right for early-stage companies and ecosystems with
existing communities. Lobo is right for companies building from scratch with
no existing community to tap into. The mistake is applying Lobo's approach
when Beh's situation applies — absorbing existing communities rather than
earning trust within them.

---

### 2. Relationship-led vs systems-led sales

**Mac Reddin** argues that the best sellers lean away from spreadsheets and
input-driven models toward human and relationship models. He says this is
harder to manage and less predictable — but that's the point.

**Mac Reddin** (in a different post, contradicting himself slightly) also
argues that warm intros need systems and training to scale — that without
a formal system, 55% of sales leaders fail to capture referral pipeline.

**My take:** This is actually the same person disagreeing with himself, which
is worth noting. The resolution is: relationships are the strategy, but
systems are the execution layer. You need both. Pure relationship-selling
doesn't scale. Pure systems-selling doesn't convert. The playbook above tries
to encode this — systematize the ask, keep the relationship human.

---

### 3. Community as growth channel vs community as product

**Patrick Woods and the Orbit Model** treat community as a growth channel —
something that feeds your pipeline, reduces churn, and amplifies your brand.
The community serves the business.

**Rosie Sherry** (Ministry of Testing) and **Jono Bacon** (People Powered)
treat community more as the product itself — something with intrinsic value
that the business serves. Sherry built a community for developers that became
a business, not the other way around.

**My take:** For most B2B SaaS companies, Woods' framing is more practical —
you need to show ROI to keep budget. But Sherry and Bacon's approach produces
stronger, more durable communities. The best answer is to start with Woods'
metrics to justify the investment, then shift toward Sherry's philosophy once
the community has momentum.

## What I Rejected and Why

### 1. The ambassador program as a primary growth lever

Several sources recommend building ambassador programs early as a way to
scale community reach. Mallory Contois at Maven and Patrick Woods both
reference ambassador-style amplification.

I rejected this as a Phase 1 strategy because ambassador programs require
an existing engaged community to draw from. Launching an ambassador program
before you have 500+ active community members is putting the cart before
the horse — you end up with ambassadors who have no community to point to,
which embarrasses both them and you.

**When to use it:** Phase 4 or later, once you have clear Contributors in
your Orbit Model who are already advocating without being asked.

---

### 2. "Don't call it community" as a long-term strategy

Patrick Woods' advice to avoid the word "community" when pitching to
skeptical executives is tactically smart. I included it in Phase 1.

But I rejected it as a long-term positioning strategy. If your company
never internally calls it community, you never build the cultural muscle
to do it well. You end up with a "customer engagement program" run by the
marketing team that produces none of the trust or compounding returns of a
real community.

Use the reframe to get budget. Then use the budget to build something real
and call it what it is.

## My Original Ideas

### AI-indexed community content as a new distribution channel

Adam DuVander's research shows that AI models like ChatGPT recommend
developer tools based on what appears in their training data — and that
rankings shift between model versions as community discussions evolve.
(Source: Adam DuVander, LinkedIn, 2025)

No source I found has connected this to community-led growth strategy
explicitly. But the implication is significant: **communities that produce
high-quality, specific, technically accurate content don't just rank in
Google anymore — they shape what AI models recommend.**

This creates a new lever for B2B SaaS community-led growth:

**The idea:** Systematically encourage your community to produce the kind
of content that gets embedded in AI training data — detailed how-to posts,
comparison threads, honest reviews, and technical discussions on platforms
like Reddit, GitHub, Stack Overflow, and LinkedIn. Not as SEO, but as
AI-index optimization.

A company with 500 active community members each producing one detailed
post per quarter generates 2,000 pieces of community-authored content per
year — content that AI models will treat as signal when recommending tools
in your category.

This is entirely unproven as a deliberate strategy, but the underlying
mechanism (AI training data reflects community discourse) is documented
by DuVander's research.

## Weaknesses of This Playbook

**1. Most sources are from 2019-2025 and the field is changing fast.**
The Orbit Model, SPACES framework, and People Powered were developed before
AI dramatically reduced the cost of content creation. The playbook assumes
human-generated community content is valuable — but if AI generates most
content by 2026, the signal-to-noise problem in communities becomes severe.

**2. This playbook works best for technical B2B SaaS.**
Developer-focused companies (Camunda, SendGrid, Orbit) have an easier time
building communities because developers already congregate online and share
knowledge freely. Non-technical B2B SaaS (HR software, finance tools) have
much harder community-building paths. This playbook doesn't adequately
address that gap.

**3. The metrics in Phase 7 are hard to attribute.**
Measuring what % of pipeline comes from community is genuinely difficult.
Most CRMs don't track this natively. The playbook recommends measuring it
without explaining how — which is a real gap.

**4. Small teams can't execute this fully.**
This playbook assumes at least one person dedicated to community. For a
5-person SaaS startup, that's rarely realistic. A version of this playbook
for solo founders or teams under 10 people is missing.
